# ai-benchmarking-and-language-performance

**Parent:** [[content/L1/ai-technical-performance-2024-2025|ai-technical-performance-2024-2025]] — The Technical Performance chapter of the AI Index Report 2025 details how AI models in 2024 achieved unprecedented benchmark gains (e.g., SWE-bench rising from 4.4% to 71.7%) and closed the gap between open-weight and closed-weight models, as well as US and Chinese models (e.g., MMLU gap narrowing from 17.5 to 0.3 percentage points). It also highlights the lauch of advanced models like OpenAI's o1 and o3, and the lauch of Meta's Llama 3.1 405B.

The Technical Performance chapter of the Artificial Intelligence Index Report 2025, published by the AI Index Steering Committee at Stanford University's Institute for Human-Centered AI, discusses the complexities of benchmarking AI intelligence. While benchmarks are effective for evaluating discrete capabilities such as vision and language (e.g., classifying an image or answering a multiple-choice question), they are more challenging to develop for multi-agent systems and human-AI interaction due to the diversity of correct answers and variability in human behaviors.

AI advances have traditionally been measured through competitions like chess and poker, which involve human-to-machine challenges. A significant milestone in AI progress is the diminishing relevance of the Turing test, originally proposed in Alan Turing’s 1950 paper “Computing Machinery and Intelligence.” The Turing test evaluates a machine’s ability to exhibit humanlike intelligence via a text-based conversation with a human judge; if the judge cannot distinguish the machine from a human, the machine passes. Recent evidence suggests that modern Large Language Models (LLMs) have advanced to the point where people struggle to differentiate them from humans, signaling that these models can now pass the Turing test.

In robotics, new models have emerged that reason about natural laws and interact with the physical world. Benchmarks such as ARMBench focus on perception tasks, while VIMA-Bench assesses robot performance in simulated environments that incorporate deep learning, communication, and perception.

Benchmarks can suffer from several deficiencies. Some are poorly constructed; a study called BetterBench analyzed 24 prominent benchmarks and found that 14 failed to report statistical significance, 17 lacked scripts for result replication, and most had inadequate documentation. BetterBench proposed a 46-criteria framework for benchmark development (covering design, implementation, documentation, and maintenance) and a public repository to improve comparability. In Figure 2.1.41 of the report, MMLU demonstrated poor adherence to quality standards, whereas GPQA performed significantly better.

Additionally, benchmarks can suffer from contamination, where LLMs encounter test questions during their training. A study by Scale found significant contamination in the performance of many LLMs on the GSM8K mathematics benchmark. To combat this, researchers introduced LiveBench, which is periodically updated with new questions from unfamiliar sources to ensure models have not seen them during training.

Because benchmark scores influence policy and procurement decisions, the AI Index sources its data from company papers, blog posts, product releases, and public repositories like RankedAGI and Papers With Code. These scores are current as of mid-February 2025, though the report notes that newer models may have been released since then. The report also operates on the assumption that scores reported by companies are factual.

In the domain of Natural Language Processing (NLP), state-of-the-art models like OpenAI’s GPT-4o, Anthropic’s Claude 3.5, and Google’s Gemini can generate coherent prose and reason across audio, images, and goal-oriented tasks. A key benchmark for English language understanding is the Massive Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU), created in 2020 by researchers from UC Berkeley, Columbia University, University of Chicago, and University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign. MMLU assesses zero-shot or few-shot performance across 57 subjects in STEM, humanities, and social sciences. OpenAI’s o1-preview achieved the highest recorded MMLU score of 92.3% in September 2024, compared to 86.4% for GPT-4 (March 2023) and 27.9% for RoBERTa in 2019, representing a 64.4 percentage point increase over five years. The human baseline for MMLU is 89.8%.

MMLU has been criticized for having overly simplistic or erroneous questions. Consequently, researchers from the University of Toronto, University of Waterloo, and Carnegie Mellon introduced MMLU-Pro in 2024, which removes trivial questions and increases answer choices. DeepSeek-R1 currently holds the highest score on MMLU-Pro at 84.0%. The report also warns that some developers use nonstandard prompting to boost MMLU scores and that reported scores can differ from academic evaluations by up to five percentage points.

For evaluating public preference, the LMSYS Chatbot Arena Leaderboard (launched in 2023) allows users to vote on anonymous model outputs. As of early 2025, the platform has over 1 million votes, with a Google Gemini model as the most preferred. The performance gap between the top model and the 10th-ranked model on the leaderboard narrowed from 11.9% (as reported in the 2024 AI Index) to 5.4% by early 2025.

To address the cost of human-curating benchmarks, UC Berkeley researchers created BenchBuilder, an automated pipeline that uses LLMs to curate open-ended prompts from crowdsourced data. The LMSYS team used BenchBuilder to develop Arena-Hard-Auto, which evaluates instruction-tuned LLMs using 500 challenging queries from the Chatbot Arena, with GPT-4 Turbo acting as the judge against a baseline model (GPT-4-0314). As of November 2024, the top scores on Arena-Hard-Auto were o1-mini (92.0), o1-preview (90.4), and Claude-3.5-Sonnet (85.2). However, the November variant of Anthropic’s Claude Sonnet 3.5 is the top model on the style leaderboard, which accounts for how response style influences user preference.
