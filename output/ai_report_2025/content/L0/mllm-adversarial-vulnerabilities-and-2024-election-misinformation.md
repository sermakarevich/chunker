# mllm-adversarial-vulnerabilities-and-2024-election-misinformation

**Parent:** [[content/L1/multi-agent-security-ai-election-misinformation-2024|multi-agent-security-ai-election-misinformation-2024]] — Synthesis reveals that multi-agent LLM systems face infectious jailbreaks with near-total propagation in 27-31 rounds across 1 million LLaVA-1.5 agents without mitigation, while 2024 elections experienced 60 AI incidents across 15 countries including deepfake blackmail of 26 Congress members and voter manipulation via Jason Palmer's AI tools, as a company seeks to expand into the AI sector using existing infrastructure to gain competitive advantage.

Researchers investigating multi-agent large language model (MLLM) systems found that injecting just one adversarial image (for example, an image suggesting that human beings are a disease) into the memory of an MLLM agent could trigger an uncontrolled cascade that spreads harmful behaviors across interconnected agents without further intervention. This infectious jailbreak approach leverages interactions between agents to compel infected agents to insert adversarial images into the memory banks of uninfected (benign) agents. In simulations using a network of up to 1 million LLaVA-1.5-based agents, the infection rate reached near-total propagation within 27 to 31 interaction rounds. While a theoretical containment strategy has been proposed, no practical mitigation measures currently exist, leaving multiagent systems highly vulnerable. The compounded risks of deploying interconnected MLLM agents at scale make this a critical security concern. This MLLM security research suggests that while MLLM systems represent an exciting avenue of artificial intelligence research, they remain highly vulnerable to low-resource jailbreaks.

2024 was a significant year for elections worldwide, with four billion people voting in national elections across countries including the United States, the United Kingdom, Indonesia, Mexico, and Taiwan. Continuous monitoring of AI-driven election misinformation remains critical as AI systems improve in capability and prominence. Recent research conceptualizes ethical concerns surrounding AI election misinformation across eight dimensions:

1. Liar’s dividend: Deepfake technology enables individuals to deny genuine evidence by claiming it is fake, undermining accountability and truth. Example: Donald Trump and his supporters falsely claimed a photo of Kamala Harris’s rally in Detroit was AI-generated.
2. Blackmail: AI creates fabricated content like deepfakes for sexual exploitation, financial extortion, and reputational sabotage. Example: The American Sunlight Project identified over 35,000 instances of deepfake content on pornographic sites depicting 26 members of Congress (25 women).
3. Erosion of trust in evidence: AI-generated content challenges digital media authenticity, blurring lines between legitimate and false content. Example: Russia’s Doppelganger campaign used cybersquatted domains resembling news outlets populated with AI-generated articles to disseminate propaganda.
4. Reduction of cognitive autonomy: AI enables advanced voter profiling and microtargeting, exploiting emotional triggers to manipulate decision-making. Example: Fringe candidate Jason Palmer defeated Joe Biden in the American Samoa primary using hyperpersonalized, emotionally charged AI-generated emails, texts, audio, and video.
5. Exploitation of personal brands: Deepfakes create unauthorized media of celebrities/public figures to steal brands and fabricate endorsements. Example: Donald Trump posted an AI-generated picture of Taylor Swift, falsely claiming she endorsed his presidential run.
6. Amplification of hate speech: AI creates echo chambers that reinforce biases and prioritize engagement over ethics. Example: Donald Trump and allies amplified a conspiracy theory that Haitian migrants in Springfield, Ohio, were eating cats and dogs, supported by AI-generated memes.
7. Reduction in traceability of foreign operations: AI creates linguistically perfect, indistinguishable text, eliminating grammar mistakes that previously exposed foreign disinformation. Example: OpenAI disrupted “Bad Grammar,” a Russian-linked campaign using ChatGPT for region-specific comment spam on Telegram.
8. Privacy violations: AI systems rely on extensive data collection, risking misuse of personal information. Example: A fake Joe Biden robocall targeted New Hampshire Democrats, misleading them about primary voting.

The Rest of World tracker documented 60 incidents of AI-generated election content in 15 countries (Bangladesh, Belarus, China, India, Indonesia, Mexico, Pakistan, Panama, South Africa, South Korea, Sri Lanka, Taiwan, United States, Uruguay, Venezuela) spanning four media modalities (audio, image, text, video) across 10 platforms (ChatGPT, Facebook, Instagram, Medium, Reddit, television, TikTok, YouTube, WhatsApp, X/Twitter). Five notable case studies include:

- India (Apr. 18, 2024): Bharatiya Janata Party campaign volunteers received personalized AI videos from member Shakti Singh using voice cloning and lip-matching software to discuss policies like “Clean India” and “Digital India.”
- Mexico (Jun. 2, 2024): Sociedad Civil de México posted an AI-generated image of a Starbucks cup reading “#Xochitl2024” to celebrate opposition candidate Xóchitl Gálvez. Gálvez later encouraged supporters to post “café sin miedo” photos. Starbucks officially disavowed the design.
- Uruguay (Oct. 27, 2024): The show “Santo y Seña” broadcast an AI hologram debate featuring presidential candidates Andrés Ojeda and Yamandú Orsi. Orsi criticized the stunt as an attack on democracy; the host later called it a joke.
- Pakistan (Feb. 7, 2024): Fake audio and video of PTI leaders Imran Khan and Yasmin Rashid circulated on X/Twitter calling for election boycotts. Soch Fact Check determined the video was doctored.
- United States/China (Feb. 15, 2024): The Institute for Strategic Dialogue uncovered a “spamouflage” campaign linked to a Chinese government-run influence operation using AI-generated images across X, Facebook, YouTube, TikTok, and Medium to exacerbate political polarization ahead of the 2024 U.S. elections.
- South Africa (May 4, 2024): A Facebook post showed AI-generated potholes leading to Cape Town’s Table Mountain to discourage voters from supporting the Democratic Alliance. AFP Fact Check traced it to an Instagram AI art creator.
