# self-driving-costs-benchmarks-safety-comparisons

**Parent:** [[content/L1/ai-evaluation-benchmarks-robotics-and-autonomous-driving-progress|ai-evaluation-benchmarks-robotics-and-autonomous-driving-progress]] — The AI Index Report 2025 details rapid advances in AI evaluation benchmarks (ARC-AGI, HLE, PlanBench, VAB, RE-Bench, GAIA), robotics foundation models (SAM2Act, Figure 02, DeepMind's AutoRT/SARA-RT, Nvidia GR00T), and autonomous driving, where Waymo achieved 150,000 weekly rides across four U.S. cities and demonstrated 88-92% fewer insurance claims than humans, while Chinese fleets scale rapidly and new closed-loop benchmarks like nuPlan and Bench2Drive replace outdated testing methods.

This section of the AI Index Report 2025 details advancements in cost-effective self-driving technology, new evaluation benchmarks, and safety comparisons between autonomous and human drivers.

The Baidu Apollo Go RT6 robotaxi carries a price tag of just $30,000 and features a battery-swapping system. This vehicle represents a major step toward making self-driving technology more cost-effective and scalable. As autonomous vehicle costs continue to decline, the adoption of self-driving cars is expected to accelerate. Notable business partnerships have also advanced autonomous technology, including Uber’s collaboration with WeRide—the world’s first publicly listed robotaxi company—to develop an autonomous ride-sharing platform in Abu Dhabi.

In 2024, researchers introduced several new benchmarks to evaluate self-driving capabilities, moving away from restrictive open-loop evaluation methods that often cause autonomous driving models to memorize driving patterns instead of learning to drive authentically. One notable example is nuPlan, developed by Motional. nuPlan is a large-scale autonomous driving dataset designed to test machine-learning-based motion planners. The benchmark includes 1,282 hours of diverse driving scenarios from multiple cities, alongside a simulation and evaluation framework that enables planners’ actions to be tested in closed-loop settings.

Bench2Drive is another new benchmark that addresses the limitations of open-loop testing by providing a comprehensive, realistic, closed-loop testing simulation environment for end-to-end autonomous vehicles (Figure 2.9.13). It includes a training set with over 2 million fully annotated frames sourced from more than 10,000 clips, as well as an evaluation suite with 220 short routes designed to test autonomous driving capabilities in diverse conditions. Figure 2.9.14 displays the driving scores of various autonomous driving methods evaluated on the Bench2Drive benchmark. The driving score accounts for both route completion and infractions, averaging route completion percentages while applying penalties based on infraction severity.

OpenAD is a recent benchmark that serves as the first real-world, open-world autonomous driving benchmark specifically for 3D object detection. OpenAD focuses on domain generalization—the ability of autonomous driving systems to adapt across diverse sensor configurations—and open-vocabulary recognition, which allows systems to identify previously unseen semantic categories.

Emerging research on self-driving car safety compares Waymo vehicles to human-driven vehicles in Phoenix and San Francisco. Figure 2.9.15 compares the number of reported incidents per million miles driven by Waymo vehicles to the estimated rates if humans had driven the same distance. The data shows that Waymo vehicles recorded significantly fewer incidents, including 1.42 fewer airbag deployments, 3.16 fewer crashes with reported injuries, and 3.65 fewer police-reported crashes per million miles driven. Figure 2.9.16 highlights the differences in incident rates across various crash locations, revealing that across all locations with available data, Waymo vehicles consistently recorded lower rates of airbag deployments, injury-reported crashes, and police-reported incidents.

Additionally, Waymo collaborated with Swiss Re, one of the world’s leading reinsurers, to conduct a study analyzing liability claims related to collisions over several million miles driven by its fully autonomous vehicles. The study compared Waymo’s liability claims to human-driver baselines derived from Swiss Re’s extensive dataset, which includes over 500,000 claims and 200 billion miles of driving data. The results showed that Waymo vehicles had an 88% reduction in property damage claims and a 92% reduction in bodily injury claims. In real terms, across 25.3 million miles driven, Waymo vehicles were involved in just nine property damage claims and two bodily injury claims, whereas human drivers over the same distance would be expected to incur 78 property damage claims and 26 bodily injury claims. Furthermore, Waymo drivers were significantly safer than latest-generation human-driven vehicles that are equipped with added safety features.
