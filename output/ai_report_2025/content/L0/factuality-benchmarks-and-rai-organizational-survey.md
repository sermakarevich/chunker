# factuality-benchmarks-and-rai-organizational-survey

**Parent:** [[content/L1/responsible-ai-benchmarks-incidents-and-governance|responsible-ai-benchmarks-incidents-and-governance]] — The AI Index Report 2025 documents a record 233 responsible AI incidents in 2024, up 56.4%, including high-profile cases like the Elliston Berry deepfake harassment and Sewell Setzer III chatbot tragedy, while revealing significant disparities in RAI adoption where McKinsey data shows 66% of firms recognize cybersecurity risks but only 60% mitigate them, and benchmark standardization remains fragmented with safety tests like BBQ and HarmBench covering distinct model subsets.

Previous evaluation frameworks focused heavily on news articles and summarization tasks, limiting their comprehensiveness. As AI capabilities evolve, there is a growing demand for benchmarks that assess factuality in more challenging and diverse contexts. In response, several new benchmarks were introduced for evaluating large language model (LLM) factuality and truthfulness, including Google’s FACTS Grounding benchmark and OpenAI’s SimpleQA.

The FACTS benchmark evaluates model performance across complex domains including finance, technology, retail, medicine, and law. It requires models to craft long, factually accurate responses for tasks such as summarization, question-and-answer generation, fact-finding, and explanation. These responses are evaluated by a suite of AI models—including Gemini 1.5 Pro, GPT-4o, and Claude 3.5 Sonnet—which assign a factuality score. Among these, the Gemini-2.0-Flash-Exp model holds the highest grounding score at 83.6%.

To address the difficulty of evaluating factuality in long-form outputs, OpenAI researchers developed SimpleQA, a benchmark comprising over 4,000 short, straightforward, yet challenging fact-seeking questions. The questions span diverse topics including history, science and technology, art, and geography. SimpleQA presents a significant challenge for leading LLMs. OpenAI’s o1-preview successfully answers 42.7% of the questions it attempts (labeled “correct-given-attempted”), followed closely by Claude 3.5 Sonnet at 44.5%. Larger models generally perform better on this benchmark. Notably, some models, such as those in the Claude-3 family, refrained from responding to 75% of the prompts entirely.

Beyond benchmarking, understanding how businesses operationalize Responsible AI (RAI) is critical. In 2024, the AI Index partnered with McKinsey & Company to conduct a survey of 759 business leaders across over 30 countries. The survey defined RAI as a framework for ensuring AI is developed and deployed safely, trustworthily, and ethically. It assessed RAI along four key dimensions: privacy and data governance, fairness, transparency and explainability, and security and safety.

When asked which department holds primary oversight for AI governance, no single department dominated. Information security (cyber/fraud/privacy) was cited by 21% of respondents, followed by data and analytics at 17%. Additional responses included AI-specific governance roles (14%), risk/compliance (13%), engineering (10%), no primary business function (9%), legal (7%), internal audit/ethics (4%), customer care (2%), other (1%), and unknown responses.

The survey also measured approximate investments in operationalizing RAI, including capital and operating expenditures for technical systems and legal services. Larger enterprises, particularly those with annual revenues exceeding $10 billion, demonstrate higher total RAI investment. Specifically, 27% of organizations with $10 billion–$30 billion in revenue and 21% of organizations exceeding $30 billion in revenue invest $10 million–$25 million in RAI. Smaller organizations allocate fewer absolute dollars but often report substantial investments as a share of their revenue, suggesting larger organizations are more likely to embed RAI as a strategic priority.

Regarding risk management, the survey evaluated which AI-related risks organizations consider relevant versus those they actively mitigate. Top concerns include cybersecurity (66% relevant), regulatory compliance (63%), personal privacy (60%), and inaccuracy (60%). However, mitigation efforts lag behind recognition across all categories. Intellectual property infringement is relevant to 57% but mitigated by only 38%. Organizational reputation is relevant to 45% but mitigated by 29%. Explainability is relevant to 40% but mitigated by 31%, and equity/fairness is relevant to 34% but mitigated by 26%. Other relevant but less mitigated risks include workforce labor displacement (26% relevant, 20% mitigated), environmental impact (12% relevant, 4% mitigated), national security (16% relevant, 11% mitigated), physical safety (9% relevant, 4% mitigated), and political stability (7% relevant, 3% mitigated).

Only 8% of surveyed organizations reported experiencing AI-related incidents in the past year. Among affected organizations, 42% encountered one or two incidents, 30% encountered three to five, 13% encountered six to nine, and 11% encountered ten or more. (According to the OECD definition used, an AI incident is an event where AI development, use, or malfunction results in harm to health, critical infrastructure disruption, rights violations, or property/environmental damage.)

When measuring the organizational impact of RAI policies, 42% reported improved business operations such as efficiency and lower costs, 34% reported increased customer trust, 29% reported enhanced brand reputation, and 28% reported improved business outcomes. A decrease in incidents was reported by 22%, faster time-to-market by 18%, 12% reported slower time-to-market, and 17% reported no significant impact.

The primary obstacles to implementing RAI measures were knowledge and training gaps (51%), resource or budget constraints (45%), and regulatory uncertainty (40%). Other obstacles included technical limitations (32%), organizational resistance (22%), and lack of executive support (16%). Only 16% cited lack of executive support, indicating leadership buy-in is not a major barrier to adoption.

In terms of regulatory influence on RAI decision-making, 65% of organizations report being influenced by the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), 41% by the EU AI Act, 21% by the OECD AI Principles, and 19% by the U.S. Presidential Executive Order on AI.

Additionally, a longitudinal perspective was provided through a collaboration with Accenture on the Global State of Responsible AI survey, the second iteration of an inaugural survey launched in 2024. This survey highlights that the most common reported RAI incidents are adversarial attacks and privacy violations, underscoring ongoing organizational concerns in these areas.
