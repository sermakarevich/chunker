# ai-training-costs-hardware-efficiency

**Parent:** [[content/L1/ai-index-report-2025-training-costs-environment-community|ai-index-report-2025-training-costs-environment-community]] — The AI Index Report 2025 details the rise in AI training costs—with Llama 3.1-405B costing an estimated $170 million—and carbon emissions, which rose from 0.01 tons for AlexNet to 8,930 tons for Llama 3.1-405B. It also highlights the growth of the AI research community, including NeurIPS attracting nearly 20,000 participants in 2024, and AI-related GitHub projects growing to 4.3 million by 2024.

The Artificial Intelligence Index Report 2025, published by the AI Index Steering Committee at Stanford University's Institute for Human-Centered AI, provides detailed analysis on the training costs and hardware requirements of notable AI models. 

Regarding training costs, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman indicated that training GPT-4 exceeded $100 million, while Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei noted in July 2024 that model training runs costing around $1 billion were already underway. In contrast, more recent models such as DeepSeek-V3 reportedly cost less, approximately $6 million, although some reports dispute this figure, arguing that actual development costs were significantly higher when factoring in employee salaries, capital expenditures, and research expenses. To refine these estimates, the AI Index partnered with Epoch AI, which analyzed factors such as training duration, hardware type, quantity, and utilization rates using data from academic publications, press releases, and technical reports.

Epoch AI's estimates, based on cloud compute rental prices, show that training costs have significantly increased over time. For example, the original Transformer model (2017) cost approximately $670 to train, RoBERTa Large (2019) cost approximately $160,000, and OpenAI’s GPT-4 (2023) was estimated at around $79 million. For 2024 models, Llama 3.1-405B had an estimated training cost of $170 million. The AI Index notes a direct correlation between the training costs of AI models and their computational requirements, where models with greater computational needs cost substantially more to train.

Hardware advancements, particularly the development of more powerful and efficient GPUs (graphics processing units), have been critical in driving AI progress by accelerating complex computations and reducing training time. Using data from Epoch AI, the AI Index reports that machine learning hardware performance, measured in 16-bit floating-point operations, grew at an annual rate of approximately 43% between 2008 and 2024, doubling every 1.9 years. This progress is driven by increased transistor counts, semiconductor manufacturing advancements, and specialized AI hardware. Precision levels vary by use case: FP32 provides the highest precision for scientific computing, while lower-precision formats like Tensor-FP16/BF16 and FP16 are optimized for speed and energy efficiency, which is ideal for edge or mobile devices.

Specifically analyzing Nvidia data center GPUs, the H100 GPU (announced March 2022) achieves 22 billion FLOP per second per dollar, which is 1.7 times the price-performance of the A100 (June 2020) and 16.9 times that of the P100 (April 2016). Epoch AI estimates that the cost of hardware with a fixed performance level decreases by 30% annually. Regarding usage, the A100 was the most commonly reported hardware used to train notable AI models, used by 64 models, followed by the V100; however, 15 models were reported as trained on the H100 by the end of 2024.

Energy efficiency, measured in FLOP/s per watt, has also improved by approximately 40% per year. For example, the Nvidia B100 (March 2024) achieved an energy efficiency of 2.5 trillion FLOP/s per watt, making it 33.8 times more energy efficient than the Nvidia P100 (April 2016), which reported 74 billion FLOP/s per watt. Despite these hardware efficiency gains, the overall power consumption required to train frontier AI models is doubling annually because of the trend of training on larger datasets. For instance, the original Transformer (2017) consumed an estimated 4,500 watts, PaLM consumed 2.6 million watts, and Llama 3.1-405B (summer 2024) required 25.3 million watts—over 5,000 times the power of the original Transformer. This increase in power consumption has led to a corresponding rise in carbon emissions, influenced by factors such as model parameters, data center power usage effectiveness (PUE), and grid carbon intensity.
