# ai-performance-convergence-open-closed-us-china-and-benchmarking-trends

**Parent:** [[content/L1/ai-convergence-benchmarking-evolution-2025|ai-convergence-benchmarking-evolution-2025]] — By early 2025, performance gaps between closed/open models and U.S./Chinese models collapsed to 1.7% on Chatbot Arena and MMLU gaps narrowed to 0.1 points, while efficient reasoning models like o1/o3 demonstrated massive gains on MATH and ARC-AGI benchmarks, establishing new evaluation frameworks such as Arena-Hard-Auto and WildBench amid rising concerns over benchmark saturation and contamination.

This section details Chapter 2 (Technical Performance), Section 2.1 (Overview of AI in 2024) from the AI Index Report 2025, focusing on the rapid narrowing of performance gaps across model openness, geography, and scale, alongside evolving benchmarking practices.

**Closed vs. Open-Weight Models**
The performance gap between AI and humans is shrinking rapidly. Even in areas where human performance previously surpassed AI, the gap between closed-weight and open-weight large language models (LLMs) is narrowing. Figure 2.1.34 illustrates this trend on the Chatbot Arena Leaderboard, a public platform for benchmarking LLM performance. In early January 2024, the leading closed-weight model outperformed the top open-weight model by 8.0%. By February 2025, this gap had narrowed to 1.7%.

On the MATH benchmark for competition-level mathematics, state-of-the-art AI systems now lead human performance by 7.9 percentage points, a significant improvement from the 0.3-point gap in 2024. On MMMU, a benchmark for complex, multidisciplinary, expert-level questions, the best 2024 model (o1) scored 78.2%, only 4.4 points below the human benchmark of 82.6%. Conversely, at the end of 2023, Google Gemini scored 59.4%, illustrating the rapid advancements in AI performance on cognitively demanding tasks. The same narrowing trend is evident across other question-answering benchmarks. In 2023, closed-weight models consistently outperformed open-weight counterparts on nearly every major benchmark: MMLU, HumanEval, MMMU, and MATH. By 2024, the gap narrowed significantly. In late 2023, closed-weight models led open models on MMLU by 15.9 points, but by the end of 2024, that difference shrank to just 0.1 percentage point. This rapid improvement was largely driven by Meta’s summer 2024 release of Llama 3.1, followed by the launch of other high-performing open-weight models such as DeepSeek’s V3.

AI models can be released with different levels of openness. Certain models, like Google’s Med-Gemini, remain entirely closed and accessible only to their developers. Meanwhile, models such as OpenAI’s GPT-4o and Anthropic’s Claude 3.5 provide limited public access through APIs. However, weights for these models are not released, preventing independent modification or thorough public scrutiny. In contrast, weights for Meta’s Llama 3.3 and Stable Video 4D are fully available, allowing anyone to modify and use them freely. Perspectives on open versus closed-weight AI models are sharply divided. Advocates of open-weight models highlight their potential to reduce market monopolies, spur innovation, improve security and robustness, and enhance transparency within the AI ecosystem. For example, Meta’s Llama models have been leveraged to create tools like Meditron, power military applications, and drive the development of numerous open-weight models worldwide. However, critics warn that open-weight models pose significant security risks, including the spread of disinformation and the creation of bioweapons, arguing for a more cautious and controlled approach.

(Note: Benchmark data was collected in early January 2025, so individual scores may have improved since publication. Regarding terminology, "open source" grants users the right to freely use, study, modify, and distribute software and source code. Open-weight models are more accessible than closed-weight models but are not necessarily fully open source, as underlying code or training data is often withheld.)

**United States vs. China Technical Performance**
The United States has historically dominated AI research and model development, with China consistently ranking second. Recent evidence suggests the landscape is rapidly changing, with China-based models catching up to their U.S. counterparts. On the LMSYS Chatbot Arena, the top U.S. model outperformed the best Chinese model by 9.3% in January 2024. By February 2025, this gap had narrowed to just 1.7%. At the end of 2023, performance gaps on benchmarks such as MMLU, MMMU, MATH, and HumanEval between U.S. and Chinese models were 17.5, 13.5, 24.3, and 31.6 percentage points, respectively. By the end of 2024, these differences had narrowed significantly to just 0.3, 8.1, 1.6, and 3.7 percentage points. The launch of DeepSeek-R1 garnered attention for another reason: the company reported achieving its results using only a fraction of the hardware resources typically required to train such a model. Beyond impacting U.S. stock markets, DeepSeek’s R1 launch raised doubts about the effectiveness of U.S. semiconductor export controls.

**Improved Performance From Smaller Models**
Recent AI progress has been driven by scaling—the idea that increasing model size and training data improves performance. While scaling has significantly boosted AI capabilities, a notable recent trend is the emergence of smaller high-performing models. Figure 2.1.38 illustrates the reduction in size of the smallest model that scores above 60% on MMLU, a widely used language model benchmark. For context, early models powering ChatGPT, such as GPT-3.5 Turbo, scored around 70% on MMLU. In 2022, the smallest model surpassing 60% on MMLU was PaLM, with 540 billion parameters. By 2024, Microsoft’s Phi-3 Mini, with just 3.8 billion parameters, achieved the same threshold, marking a 142-fold reduction in model size over two years.

2024 was a breakthrough year for smaller AI models. Nearly every major AI developer released compact, high-performing models, including GPT-4o mini, o1-mini, Gemini 2.0 Flash, Llama 3.1 8B, and Mistral Small. The rise of small models is significant for several reasons. It demonstrates increasing algorithmic efficiency, allowing developers to achieve more with less data and at lower training costs. These efficiency gains, combined with growing datasets, could lead to even higher-performing models. Additionally, inference on smaller models is typically faster and less expensive. Their emergence also lowers the barrier to entry for AI developers and businesses looking to integrate AI into their operations.

**Model Performance Convergence at the Frontier**
In recent years, AI model performance at the frontier has converged, with multiple providers now offering highly capable models. This marks a shift from late 2022, when ChatGPT’s launch—widely seen as AI’s breakthrough into public consciousness—coincided with a landscape dominated by just two major players: OpenAI and Google. OpenAI, founded in 2015, released GPT-3 in 2020, while Google introduced models like PaLM and Chinchilla in 2022. Since then, new players have entered the scene, including Meta with its Llama models, Anthropic with Claude, High-Flyer’s DeepSeek, Mistral with Le Chat, and xAI with Grok. As competition has intensified, model performance has increasingly converged. The performance gap between the highest- and 10th-ranked models on the Chatbot Arena Leaderboard fell from 11.9% to 5.4% by early 2025. Similarly, the difference between the top two models fell from 4.9% in 2023 to just 0.7% in 2024. This validates 2023 predictions that AI companies lack a technological moat to shield them from rivals.

**Benchmarking AI**
For years, the AI Index has used benchmarks to monitor the technical progress of AI systems over time. While benchmarks remain a key tool, it is important to acknowledge their limitations and guide the community toward more effective benchmarking practices. As noted in last year’s AI Index, many prominent AI benchmarks are reaching saturation. With AI systems advancing rapidly, even newly designed, more challenging tests often remain relevant for only a few years. Some experts suggest that the era of new academic benchmarks may be coming to an end. Games with a physical component or team capabilities are also good measures of progress for AI, and the robotics community has embarked on challenging game competitions such as RoboCup for soccer-playing robots. Another area where competitions are used involves coordination and teamwork, where multi-agent systems demonstrate advances in distributed reasoning.

Historically, the AI community developed benchmarks to evaluate different approaches against the same gold standard. Significant advances in AI have been possible because models with diverse data in different domains could be evaluated automatically by third parties without releasing the test data. However, when model developers release new models, they typically report benchmark scores that are often accepted at face value. This approach has flaws. In some cases, companies use nonstandard prompting techniques, making model-to-model comparisons unreliable. To truly assess the capabilities of AI systems, more rigorous and comprehensive evaluations are needed.
