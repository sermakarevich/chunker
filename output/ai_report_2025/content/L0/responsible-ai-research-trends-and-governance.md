# responsible-ai-research-trends-and-governance

**Parent:** [[content/L1/responsible-ai-benchmarks-incidents-and-governance|responsible-ai-benchmarks-incidents-and-governance]] — The AI Index Report 2025 documents a record 233 responsible AI incidents in 2024, up 56.4%, including high-profile cases like the Elliston Berry deepfake harassment and Sewell Setzer III chatbot tragedy, while revealing significant disparities in RAI adoption where McKinsey data shows 66% of firms recognize cybersecurity risks but only 60% mitigate them, and benchmark standardization remains fragmented with safety tests like BBQ and HarmBench covering distinct model subsets.

Since 2019, academic conference submissions on transparency and explainability have increased fourfold, reaching 355 submissions in 2024 across AAAI, FAccT, AIES, ICML, ICLR, and NeurIPS. AI security and safety submissions to select conferences nearly doubled over the past year, rising from 276 to 521, reflecting the growing emphasis on these areas among responsible AI researchers.

In 2024, global cooperation on AI governance intensified, shifting from isolated national initiatives to coordinated international frameworks. Key responsible AI policymaking milestones include:
- May 2024: The OECD updated its AI principles to emphasize inclusive growth, transparency, explainability, the rule of law, human rights, and democratic values.
- May 2024: The Council of Europe adopted the Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy, and the Rule of Law, a legally binding treaty aligning AI lifecycle activities with human rights and democratic values.
- June 2024: The European Union passed the EU AI Act, the first comprehensive regulatory framework for AI in a major economy, which categorizes AI by risk and places primary obligations on high-risk system providers or developers.
- July 2024: The African Union launched its Continental AI Strategy, promoting unified, ethical, responsible, and equitable AI development across Africa.
- September 2024: The United Nations updated its Governing AI for Humanity report, recommending a blueprint for addressing AI risks and urging collaboration among standards organizations, technology companies, civil society, and policymakers.
- October 2024: The G7 reaffirmed commitments to fair and open AI markets and coordinated regulatory approaches.
- October 2024: Following the 12th ASEAN-United States Summit, ASEAN and U.S. leaders issued a statement committing to cooperative international AI governance frameworks and standards.
- November 2024: The International Network of AI Safety Institutes was established, uniting nine countries and the EU to advance AI safety, understand advanced AI risks, and propose solutions.
- February 2025: The Arab League launched a dialogue circle on Artificial Intelligence in the Arab World, focusing on innovative applications and ethical challenges.

The AI Index defines privacy as an individual’s right to the confidentiality, anonymity, and protection of personal data, including the right to consent to and be informed about data use. This definition also encompasses an organization’s responsibility to protect these rights if it collects or uses personal data, alongside the individual’s right to correct misrepresented information. In AI applications, privacy requires ensuring personal data is handled respectfully, protected from exposure, and collected/processed transparently in compliance with regulations like the GDPR. Data governance refers to the policies, procedures, and standards organizations implement to ensure data quality, security, and ethical use, which is particularly critical for sensitive or personally identifiable information.

A 2025 large-scale audit by Longpre et al. examined over 1,800 text datasets widely used for training foundation models and identified systemic issues in dataset licensing and attribution. More than 70% of datasets on popular hosting sites lacked adequate license information, and 50% of licenses were miscategorized. The researchers categorized licenses into commercial, unspecified, noncommercial, and academic-only, revealing sharp discrepancies between their classifications and those reported by GitHub, Papers with Code, and Hugging Face. License misattribution creates legal and ethical risks, including potential copyright violations, unfair compensation for data creators, and model biases from excluded licensed data. It also hinders transparency, accountability, and reproducibility in AI research, complicating efforts to verify or audit training data. These findings underscore the need for clear documentation, improved standards, and responsible licensing practices.

Concurrently, a longitudinal audit of 14,000 web domains used in AI training datasets (C4, RefinedWeb, and Dolma) revealed a sharp increase in data use restrictions between 2023 and 2024. Many websites implemented new protocols to limit data scraping for AI training through robots.txt updates and terms-of-service modifications. The proportion of tokens in the top C4 web domains subject to full restrictions rose from 10% in 2017 to 48% in 2024, with a 25 percentage point increase occurring solely between 2023 and 2024. Terms-of-service restriction categories across the top C4 domains also showed shifting trends from 2016 to 2024. This diminishing consent aligns with legal disputes over fair use, such as the New York Times lawsuit against OpenAI. OpenAI’s web crawlers encounter the highest restriction levels, while smaller developers face fewer barriers. The audit highlighted inconsistent enforcement due to ineffective robots.txt signaling and mismatches between stated and enforced policies. These dynamics indicate a decline in publicly available web data for AI training, which may threaten data diversity, model alignment, and scalability, as recent performance improvements heavily depend on large-scale datasets and restrictive policies could impede future model scaling.
