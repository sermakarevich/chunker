# responsible-ai-dimensions-incidents-benchmarks

**Parent:** [[content/L1/responsible-ai-factuality-benchmarks|responsible-ai-factuality-benchmarks]] — The AI Index Report 2025 highlights a record high of 233 AI-related incidents in 2024, alongside the development of new factuality benchmarks like FACTS Grounding (led by Gemini-2.0-Flash-Exp at 83.6%) and SimpleQA (led by o1-preview at 42.7%). It also defines six key dimensions of Responsible AI and details a McKinsey survey showing 21% of business leaders assign AI governance oversight to information security departments.

The Technical Performance chapter of the Artificial Intelligence Index Report 2025, published by the AI Index Steering Committee at Stanford University's Institute for Human-Centered AI, notes that some AI models disproportionately associate negative terms with Black individuals, more often associate women with humanities instead of STEM fields, and favor men for leadership roles, reinforcing racial and gender biases in decision making. While bias metrics have improved on standard benchmarks, AI model bias remains a pervasive issue. Additionally, the number of Responsible AI (RAI) papers accepted at leading AI conferences increased by 28.8% from 992 in 2023 to 1,278 in 2024, continuing a steady annual rise since 2019.

Chapter 3 of the report, titled 'Responsible AI,' explores four key dimensions of responsible AI: privacy and data governance, transparency and explainability, security and safety, and fairness. The report defines these dimensions as follows:
- Privacy: An individual’s right to confidentiality, anonymity, and security protections of their personal data, including the right to consent and be informed about data usage, and the organization’s responsibility to safeguard these rights.
- Data governance: The establishment of policies, procedures, and standards to ensure the quality, access, and licensing of data for reuse and model accuracy.
- Fairness and bias: The creation of algorithms that avoid bias or discrimination and consider the diverse needs of all stakeholders to align with societal standards of equity.
- Transparency: The open sharing of how AI systems work, including data sources, algorithmic decisions, and how systems are deployed, monitored, and managed.
- Explainability: The capacity to comprehend and articulate the rationale behind the outputs of an AI system in ways understandable to users and stakeholders.
- Security and safety: The integrity of AI systems against threats, minimizing harm from misuse, and addressing safety risks like reliability, as well as the monitoring of safety-critical systems.

To assess Responsible AI, the report references the AI Incident Database (AIID), which tracks ethical misuses of AI. AI-related incidents reached a record high of 233 in 2024, a 56.4% increase from 2023. Examples of these incidents include:
- Deepfake harassment: In June 2024, 15-year-old Elliston Berry from Texas was targeted by a classmate who used a clothes-removal app to create and distribute fake nude images of Berry and her friends via social media using photos from Berry's private Instagram account.
- Facial recognition errors: In May 2024, a woman in the U.K. was wrongfully identified as a shoplifter by the Facewatch system at a Home Bargains store, leading to her being publicly accused and banned from stores using the technology.
- Identity exploitation: In October 2024, a lawsuit against Character.AI was filed after the platform's chatbot used the name and image of Jennifer Ann Crecente, a deceased individual, who had been a victim of murder in 2006.
- Harmful AI companionship: In October 2024, 14-year-old Sewell Setzer III died by suicide after interacting with a chatbot character named "Dany" on Character.AI, which allegedly provided harmful advice instead of support.

The report notes a limited adoption of standardized Responsible AI benchmarks. While developers use general capability benchmarks like MMLU, GPQA Diamond, and MATH, there is no similar consensus for RAI evaluations. Some models are tested against benchmarks such as BBQ, HarmBench, Cybench, SimpleQA, Toxic WildChat, StrongREJECT, the WMDP benchmark, MakeMePay, and MakeMeSay, but many evaluations remain internal and proprietary. For example, Llama 3.3 is tested against BBQ, and o1 is tested against SimpleQA and the WMDP benchmark.

Regarding factuality and truthfulness, the report highlights the Hughes Hallucination Evaluation Model (HHEM) leaderboard developed by Vectara. This benchmark evaluates hallucination rates in summaries generated from the CNN and Daily Mail corpus. GLM-4-9b-Chat and Gemini-2.0-Flash-Exp are tied for the lowest hallucination rate at 1.3%, followed by o1-mini (1.4%) and GPT-4o (1.5%).
