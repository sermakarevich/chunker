# notable-ai-models-trends

**Parent:** [[content/L1/ai-research-patents-models-trends|ai-research-patents-models-trends]] — The AI Index Report 2025 reveals a China-led surge in AI research publications (23.2% share) and patents (69.7% share) by 2023, while the U.S. leads in notable AI models (40 in 2024) and top-cited research. The report also highlights a training compute doubling every five months and a massive reduction in inference costs, such as GPT-3.5 level performance costs falling from $20 to $0.07 per million tokens.

The Artificial Intelligence Index Report 2025, published by the AI Index Steering Committee at Stanford University's Institute for Human-Centered AI, provides detailed data on notable AI models from 2014 to 2024. According to data from Epoch AI, the organizations that produced the most notable AI models in this period were Google (187 models), Meta (82), Microsoft (39), OpenAI (36), Carnegie Mellon University (25), Stanford University (25), Tsinghua University (22), UC Berkeley (22), Nvidia (17), the University of Oxford (16), MIT (15), Salesforce (15), the University of Washington (15), Alibaba (14), and the Allen Institute for AI (12). Research published by DeepMind is classified under Google in these figures. 

Notable AI models are released under various access types. API access allows users to interact with models via queries without direct access to weights; Hosted access (no API) refers to models available through a platform interface without programmatic access (e.g., Gemini 2.0 Pro); Open weights (restricted use) provide weights but impose limitations, such as prohibiting commercial use or redistribution (e.g., DeepSeek-V3); Open weights (unrestricted) are fully open, allowing free use, modification, and redistribution (e.g., AlphaGeometry); Open weights (noncommercial) share weights but restrict use to research or noncommercial purposes (e.g., Mistral Large 2); and unreleased models remain private (e.g., ESM3 98B). The designation 'unknown' is used for models with unclear access types. In 2024, API access was the most common release type, with 20 of 61 models made available this way. Over time, API access has seen a steady rise since 2020; in 2024, 32.8% of notable AI models were released via API access, followed by Open weights (restricted use) at 18.03%, Unreleased at 16.39%, Open weights (unrestricted) at 11.48%, Open weights (noncommercial) at 9.84%, and Hosted access (no API) at 8.20%, with 3.28% classified as Unknown.

Regarding training code, 60.7% of notable AI models launched in 2024 were released without corresponding training code, contrasting with traditional open-source software where all components are typically made available.

Model parameters—numerical values learned during training—have risen sharply since the early 2010s. High-parameter models typically outperform those with fewer parameters but require more data and financial resources for training, a trend particularly evident in the industry sector. The training dataset sizes for Large Language Models (LLMs) double approximately every eight months. For example, the Transformer model (2017) was trained on approximately 2 billion tokens, GPT-3 175B (davinci) was trained on an estimated 374 billion tokens, and Meta’s Llama 3.3 (2024) was trained on roughly 15 trillion tokens.

Training times have also increased. AlexNet (2012) trained in five to six days on less advanced hardware, whereas Llama 3.1-405B required approximately 90 days to train, and Google’s Gemini 1.0 Ultra (2023) took around 100 days.

Compute refers to the computational resources required to train and operate a model. Training compute for notable AI models doubles roughly every five months, a trend especially pronounced in the last five years. For instance, AlexNet (2012) required an estimated 470 petaFLOP, the original Transformer (2017) required around 7,400 petaFLOP, and GPT-4o required 38 billion petaFLOP.
