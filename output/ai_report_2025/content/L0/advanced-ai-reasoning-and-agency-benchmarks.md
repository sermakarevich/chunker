# advanced-ai-reasoning-and-agency-benchmarks

**Parent:** [[content/L1/ai-evaluation-benchmarks-robotics-and-autonomous-driving-progress|ai-evaluation-benchmarks-robotics-and-autonomous-driving-progress]] — The AI Index Report 2025 details rapid advances in AI evaluation benchmarks (ARC-AGI, HLE, PlanBench, VAB, RE-Bench, GAIA), robotics foundation models (SAM2Act, Figure 02, DeepMind's AutoRT/SARA-RT, Nvidia GR00T), and autonomous driving, where Waymo achieved 150,000 weekly rides across four U.S. cities and demonstrated 88-92% fewer insurance claims than humans, while Chinese fleets scale rapidly and new closed-loop benchmarks like nuPlan and Bench2Drive replace outdated testing methods.

The AI Index Report 2025 details advanced evaluation benchmarks focused on generalized learning, automated reasoning, and AI agency. To properly assess AI capabilities, researchers emphasize that evaluation must encompass the capacity for general learning and skill acquisition, describing artificial general intelligence as a system 'capable of efficiently acquiring new skills and solving novel problems for which it was neither designed nor trained.'

**ARC-AGI Benchmark**
ARC-AGI is a benchmark introduced in 2019 by François Chollet, the creator of the Keras deep-learning framework, to measure generalized learning. It is impossible for systems to prepare in advance for this benchmark because each task introduces a unique logic. The tasks require no specialized world knowledge or language skills but instead draw on assumed prior knowledge, such as the concept of objects, basic topology, and elementary arithmetic—concepts typically mastered by children at an early age. When first run in 2020, the top-performing system on ARC-AGI achieved a score of only 20%. Four years later, in 2024, this score had risen to just 33%. In 2025, OpenAI’s o3 model achieved a score of 75.7%. When o3 was allocated a high-compute budget exceeding the benchmark’s $10,000 limit, it achieved a score of 87.5% on ARC-AGI-1 using the private evaluation set.

**Humanity’s Last Exam (HLE)**
As many popular AI benchmarks reach saturation, researchers behind the MMLU benchmark recently introduced Humanity’s Last Exam (HLE), a new benchmark comprising 2,700 highly challenging questions across dozens of subject areas. The dataset features multimodal questions contributed by subject matter experts, including leading professors and graduate-level reviewers, that resist simple internet lookups or database retrieval. Each question was tested against state-of-the-art LLMs before inclusion; if an existing model could answer it, the question was rejected. Initial testing indicates that HLE remains highly challenging for current AI systems. Even top models, such as OpenAI’s o1, score just 8.8%. Accuracy scores on the benchmark are as follows: GPT-4o achieved 3.10%, Grok-2 achieved 3.90%, Claude 3.5 Sonnet achieved 4.80%, Gemini 1.5 Pro achieved 5.20%, Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking achieved 7.20%, and o1 achieved 8.80%. Researchers behind HLE are closely monitoring how quickly LLMs improve, and they speculate that performance could exceed 50% by the end of 2025.

**Planning and PlanBench**
Planning is an intelligent task that involves reasoning about actions that alter the world, requiring consideration of hypothetical future states, including potential external actions and other transformative events. A group from Arizona State University proposed PlanBench, a benchmark suite containing problems used in the automated planning community, especially those used in the International Planning Competition. PlanBench tests models on 600 problems in which a hand tries to construct stacks of blocks when it is only allowed to move one block at a time to a table or to the top of a clear block. Released in 2022, the benchmark initially demonstrated that models like GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 struggled with planning tasks. OpenAI’s o1 showed significant improvements on PlanBench, though it still struggles with reliable and consistent planning. In the Blocksworld zero-shot evaluation domain, o1 achieved a score of 97.8%, far surpassing the next best LLM, Llama 3.1 405B (62.6%), and dramatically outperforming GPT-4o (35.5%). In the more challenging Mystery Blocksworld domain, where some answers are syntactically obfuscated, o1 scored 52.8% zero-shot, compared to just 0.8% for Llama 3.1 405B and 0.0% for GPT-4. Planning is a combinatorial problem, and solving problems with long solutions is expected to take more than linear time. Not surprisingly, when tested on instances that require at least 20 steps, o1 manages to solve just 23.6%. The release of OpenAI’s o1 was met with enthusiasm from the AI research community, as it was designed to actively reason rather than function purely as an autoregressive large language model.

**AI Agents and VisualAgentBench**
AI agents, which are autonomous or semiautonomous systems designed to operate within specific environments to accomplish goals, represent a significant frontier in AI research with applications ranging from academic research assistance and meeting scheduling to online shopping and vacation booking. For decades, the topic has been widely discussed in the AI community, yet few benchmarks have achieved widespread adoption, including AgentBench and MLAgentBench featured in previous years. This lag stems from the inherent complexity of benchmarking agentic tasks, which are typically more diverse, dynamic, and variable than tasks like image classification or answering language questions. VisualAgentBench (VAB), launched in 2024, represents a significant step forward by reflecting the growing multimodality of AI models and their proficiency in navigating virtual and embodied environments. VAB tests agents across three broad categories: embodied agents (operating in household and gaming environments), GUI agents (interacting with mobile and web applications), and visual design agents (such as CSS debugging). VAB presents a significant challenge for AI systems. The top-performing model, GPT-4o, achieves an overall success rate of just 36.2%. Other models' success rates on the VAB test set are as follows: GPT-4o-turbo achieved 29.90%, Gemini-1.5-Pro achieved 31.70%, Claude-3.5-Sonnet achieved 26.90%, GPT-4-02 achieved 20.50%, Llama-3.3-70B achieved 21.90%, GPT-4o-mini achieved 19.80%, o1-preview achieved 16.00%, GPT-4-turbo achieved 12.00%, Gemini-2.0-Flash achieved 10.50%, GPT-4 achieved 10.30%, Claude-3.5-Instant achieved 8.90%, GPT-4-01 achieved 8.40%, Llama-3.1-8B achieved 7.70%, and o1-mini achieved 6.30%. Most proprietary language models average around 20%. According to the benchmark’s authors, these results reveal that current AI models are far from ready for direct deployment in agentic settings.

**RE-Bench**
The emergence of increasingly capable agentic AI systems has fueled predictions that AI might soon take on the work of computer scientists or researchers. Until 2024, few benchmarks rigorously tested the R&D capabilities of top-performing AI systems. That year, researchers launched RE-Bench, featuring seven challenging, open-ended machine learning research environments. These tasks were informed by data from 71 eight-hour attempts by over 60 human experts, including optimizing a kernel, conducting a scaling law experiment, and fine-tuning GPT-2 for question answering. Researchers comparing humans and frontier AI models found that in short time-horizon settings, such as with a two-hour budget, the best AI systems achieve scores four times higher than human experts. However, as the time budget increases, human performance begins to surpass that of AI. With an eight-hour budget, human performance slightly exceeds AI, and with a 32-hour budget, humans outperform AI by a factor of two. The researchers also note that for certain tasks, AI agents already demonstrate expertise comparable to humans but can deliver results significantly faster and at a lower cost. For example, AI agents can write custom Triton kernels more quickly than any human expert.

**GAIA Benchmark**
GAIA is a benchmark for General AI assistants introduced by Meta in May 2024. It consists of 466 questions designed to assess AI systems’ ability to perform a broad range of tasks, including reasoning, multimodal processing, web browsing, and tool use. Unlike straightforward, exam-style questions, GAIA challenges AI models with complex, multistep problems that may require searching the open web, interpreting multimodal inputs, and reasoning through intricate scenarios. When researchers launched GAIA, they found that existing LLMs lagged significantly behind human performance. For instance, GPT-4 with plugins correctly answered only 15% of the questions, compared to 92% for human respondents. Performance on GAIA has improved rapidly. In 2024, the top system achieved a score of 65.1%, marking a roughly 30 percentage point increase from the highest score recorded in 2023.
