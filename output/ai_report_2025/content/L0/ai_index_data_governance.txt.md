**Parent:** [[content/L1/responsible-ai-governance-ethics|responsible-ai-governance-ethics]] — The AI Index Report 2025 details the establishment of the International Network of AI Safety Institutes in February 2025 and identifies that over 70% of training datasets on popular hosting sites lack adequate license information.

The Artificial Intelligence Index Report 2025, published by the AI Index Steering Committee at Stanford University's Institute for Human-Centered AI, provides updates on global AI safety cooperation and the ethics of AI. In February 2025, the first International Network of AI Safety Institutes was established, uniting the European Union and nine countries. This network consists of technical organizations dedicated to advancing AI safety, proposing solutions, and helping societies and governments understand the risks associated with advanced AI systems. Additionally, the Arab League launched the Arab Dialogue Circle on “Artificial Intelligence in the Arab World: Innovative Applications and Ethical Challenges† at its headquarters, focusing on AI innovations and ethical considerations.

Within Chapter 3, 'Responsible AI,' the report addresses Privacy and Data Governance. The AI Index defines privacy as an individual’s right to confidentiality, anonymity, and the protection of their personal data, including the right to be informed about and consent to how their data is used. Privacy also encompasses an organization's responsibility to safeguard these rights when collecting, storing, or using personal data and includes the individual's right to correct misrepresented sensitive information. In the context of AI, this requires handling personal data to respect privacy rights, protecting sensitive information from exposure, and ensuring transparency and compliance with laws such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Data governance refers to the policies, procedures, and standards an organization establishes to ensure the quality, security, and ethical use of data both within and outside the organization, particularly regarding the accuracy, fairness, and consensual use of sensitive or personally identifiable information (PII).

Featured research on data governance includes a large-scale audit of over 1,800 text datasets used for training foundation models. The researchers found that more than 70% of datasets on popular hosting sites lacked adequate license information, and 50% of the licenses were miscategorized. The researchers compared their own license labels (commercial, unspecified, noncommercial, and academic-only) against classifications from GitHub, Papers with Code, and Hugging Face, finding sharp differences. This lack of clarity in licensing can impact the legality and transparency of model training.

Furthermore, a study on data scraping and consent found that many websites now prohibit AI scraping. The report notes that the prevalence of 'AI.txt' files (which signal that a site is not for AI training) has increased, indicating a shift toward protecting data from unauthorized use in training sets.

Additionally, the report discusses the risk of 'data contamination,' where training data includes test set examples, potentially inflating performance metrics. It emphasizes the need for rigorous data cleaning and the use of 'canary' strings to identify and prevent the leakage of sensitive or test data into training sets.

Regarding data and consent, the report highlights a trend where website owners are increasingly using robots.txt or AI.txt to block AI crawlers, reflecting a growing tension between the need for massive datasets and the right to control one's data.

In terms of fairness and bias, the report addresses the issue of 'representational harm,' where certain groups are depicted in stereotypical or harmful ways. It notes that while some efforts have been made to filter out biased content, the process can be imperfect, and some biases remain embedded in training data.

Finally, the report discusses the issue of 'algorithmic bias' in a broader context, noting that without diverse and representative training sets, models may perform poorly or unfairly for marginalized groups, further reinforcing the need for transparency in data sourcing and curation.

Regarding the technical aspects of data governance, the report highlights that the use of 'canary' strings—unique, identifiable sequences of text—can help developers detect when a dataset has been leaked into a training set, allowing for better control over data provenance.
