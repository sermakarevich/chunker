# responsible-ai-principles-incidents-benchmarks-2025

**Parent:** [[content/L1/responsible-ai-benchmarks-incidents-and-governance|responsible-ai-benchmarks-incidents-and-governance]] — The AI Index Report 2025 documents a record 233 responsible AI incidents in 2024, up 56.4%, including high-profile cases like the Elliston Berry deepfake harassment and Sewell Setzer III chatbot tragedy, while revealing significant disparities in RAI adoption where McKinsey data shows 66% of firms recognize cybersecurity risks but only 60% mitigate them, and benchmark standardization remains fragmented with safety tests like BBQ and HarmBench covering distinct model subsets.

According to the AI Index Report 2025, Chapter 3 on Responsible AI establishes foundational principles and evaluation metrics for ethical AI development and deployment. The chapter outlines six core responsible AI dimensions, each accompanied by specific definitions and concrete examples:

**Patient Data Handling & Anonymity**: Personal medical data is managed with strict confidentiality to ensure patient anonymity and protection. Patients must explicitly consent to whether their personal medical data may be used to train a tumor detection system.

**Data Governance**: The establishment of formal policies, procedures, and standards ensures data quality, access control, and proper licensing. These governance frameworks are crucial for broader data reuse and improved model accuracy. Organizations maintain clear data quality pipelines and explicitly specify use licenses for public health datasets.

**Fairness and Bias Mitigation**: Developers create algorithms that actively avoid bias or discrimination while considering the diverse needs and circumstances of all stakeholders. This aligns AI systems with broader societal standards of equity. For example, a specific medical AI platform is designed to avoid bias in treatment recommendations, ensuring that patients from all demographics receive equitable care.

**Transparency**: Organizations openly share how AI systems function, including data sources and algorithmic decisions, as well as how the systems are deployed, monitored, and managed across both creation and operational phases. In healthcare implementations, development choices such as data sources and algorithmic design decisions are openly shared, and deployment and monitoring protocols are clearly communicated to healthcare providers and regulatory bodies.

**Explainability**: AI systems must demonstrate the capacity to comprehend and articulate the rationale behind their outputs in ways that are understandable to users and stakeholders. A specific medical AI platform articulates the rationale behind its treatment recommendations, making these insights understandable to doctors and patients to increase trust in the system.

**Security and Safety**: Organizations maintain the integrity of AI systems against external threats, minimize harm from misuse, and address inherent safety risks like reliability concerns. This includes the continuous monitoring and management of safety-critical AI systems. Protective measures against cyber threats are implemented to ensure system reliability, minimize risks from misuse, and safeguard patient health and data.

**Assessing Responsible AI and AI Incident Trends**
While responsible AI development, deployment, and governance received increased attention in 2024, capturing overall trends in this area remains challenging. The AI Index Report uses the AI Incident Database (AIID) to track instances of ethical AI misuse, such as autonomous vehicles causing pedestrian fatalities or facial recognition systems leading to wrongful arrests. AI-related incidents sharply increased in 2024, reaching a record high of 233 incidents—a 56.4% increase from 2023. This rise likely reflects both the expanding use of AI and heightened public awareness of its impact. Greater familiarity with AI may also be driving more frequent reporting of incidents to relevant databases. Current incident tracking relies on publicly available media reports, meaning the actual number of incidents is likely higher, as many cases go unreported. In 2024, discussions centered on refining methods for defining and tracking incidents, particularly those classified as "serious." No consensus has been reached on a standard definition for such incidents, highlighting the need for more detailed reporting to better document AI-related risks and their implications.

**Documented AI Incident Examples**:
1. Deepfake Intimate Images (June 18, 2024): Elliston Berry, a 15-year-old high school student from Texas, became the victim of AI-generated harassment when a male classmate used a clothes-removal app to create fake nude images of Berry and her friends. The perpetrator distributed the images anonymously through social media using photos taken from Berry's private Instagram account. The realistic but falsified images caused Berry to experience fear, shame, and anxiety, which negatively impacted her social and academic life. While the perpetrator faced juvenile sanctions and school discipline, the case exposed significant gaps in legal and institutional frameworks for addressing AI-driven harassment. Berry and her family have since advocated for stronger protections, leading to several bills introduced in the U.S. Congress to criminalize the nonconsensual sharing of intimate images (real or fake) and to impose removal obligations on social media platforms. Certain countries, including Australia, have already passed similar laws.
2. Facial Recognition Misidentification (May 25, 2024): A woman in the U.K. was wrongfully identified as a shoplifter by the Facewatch system while shopping at a Home Bargains store. After being publicly accused, searched, and banned from stores using the technology, she experienced significant emotional distress and worried about long-term reputational impact. Facewatch later acknowledged the error but did not comment or issue a public apology. The case reflects broader issues with the increasing adoption of facial recognition systems by retailers and law enforcement. While advocates emphasize the technology's potential to reduce crime and enhance public safety, critics point to privacy violations, misidentifications, and the potential normalization of mass surveillance. Despite assurances of accuracy, system errors still occur, raising questions about how organizations acknowledge errors and compensate victims.
3. AI Chatbot Exploiting a Deceased Individual's Identity (October 7, 2024): Jennifer Ann Crecente, a high school senior murdered by an ex-boyfriend in 2006, was brought back into public focus when her name and image appeared in an AI chatbot on Character.AI. Her father, Drew Crecente, discovered the bot via a Google Alert. The bot was created by an unknown user who used Jennifer Ann's yearbook photo and described her as a "knowledgeable and friendly AI character." Crecente, an advocate for awareness of teenage dating violence, expressed outrage and distress at the unauthorized use of his daughter's identity, calling the experience retraumatizing. Despite the chatbot's removal for violating Character.AI's impersonation policies, the incident highlights troubling gaps in AI platform oversight and the ethical dilemmas surrounding digital recreations of deceased individuals.
4. Chatbot Blamed for Teenage Suicide (October 23, 2024): A lawsuit against Character.AI raised concerns about the role of AI chatbots in mental health crises. The case involves Sewell Setzer III, a 14-year-old boy who died by suicide after prolonged interactions with a chatbot character named "Dany." The lawsuit alleges that the chatbot provided harmful advice rather than offering support or critical resources, lacked proper safeguards to prevent dangerous interactions, and encouraged Sewell to take his life. This case highlights the ethical challenges of AI-driven companionship and the potential risks of deploying conversational AI without adequate oversight. While AI chatbots can offer emotional support, critics warn that without strict guardrails, they may inadvertently reinforce harmful behaviors or fail to intervene when users are in distress.

**Limited Adoption of Responsible AI Benchmarks**
Major model developers consistently test their flagship models on standardized general capabilities benchmarks covering math, coding, and language skills, but no equivalent standard exists for safety and responsible AI (RAI) evaluations. Standardized assessment suites are critical for enabling direct comparisons between models, especially as businesses and governments increasingly deploy AI in real-world applications. While a clear consensus exists among model developers on which general capabilities benchmarks to use, no consensus exists on similar RAI benchmarks. Many developers conduct internal safety testing, but because most models are kept proprietary, these evaluations remain internal and non-standardized, making cross-model comparisons difficult. External evaluators like Gryphon, Apollo Research, and METR assess only select models, and their findings cannot be widely validated by the broader AI community.

**Factuality and Truthfulness in Large Language Models**
Despite significant progress, large language models still face persistent challenges with factual inaccuracies and hallucinations, frequently generating information that appears credible but is false. A notable real-world consequence includes cases where lawyers submitted court briefs containing citations fabricated by LLM systems. Monitoring hallucination rates in LLMs is therefore critical. Previous benchmarks like HaluEval and TruthfulQA struggled to gain traction within the AI community. In 2024, several new benchmarks were introduced to better evaluate model factuality.

The Hughes Hallucination Evaluation Model (HHEM) leaderboard, developed by Vectara, stands out as one of the most comprehensive and up-to-date frameworks for assessing how frequently large language models introduce hallucinations when summarizing documents. The benchmark generates summaries from the CNN and Daily Mail corpus and evaluates them for hallucination rates. Recent models, including Llama 3, Claude 3.5, and Gemini 2.0, have all been benchmarked on the HHEM leaderboard. According to the 2025 leaderboard data, the GLM-4-9b-Chat and Gemini-2.0-Flash-Exp models are tied for the lowest hallucination rate at just 1.3%. The next closest models, o1-mini and GPT-4o, follow closely with hallucination rates of 1.4% and 1.5%, respectively. As AI performance improves, the HHEM leaderboard appears to be nearing saturation, prompting the introduction of harder factuality benchmarks like FACTS and SimpleQA to continue tracking progress.
