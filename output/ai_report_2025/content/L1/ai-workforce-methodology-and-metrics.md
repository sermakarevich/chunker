# ai-workforce-methodology-and-metrics

**Parent:** [[index]]

The analysis of the global AI labor market and workforce transition is underpinned by a rigorous methodology designed to track the proliferation of AI skills, the evolution of professional roles, and the technical parameters of the tools used to conduct these analyses. Central to this framework is the use of LinkedIn data to quantify the exact nature of professional shifts. The methodology distinguishes between two primary categories of skills: 'AI skills' and 'AI-related skills.' 'AI skills' are specifically defined as the proficiency in the use of AI tools and the actual development of AI systems. 'AI-related skills' represent a broader taxonomy, encompassing both the direct development and use of AI tools, as well as broader technical skills that support the AI ecosystem. To determine these metrics, the analysis employs specific taxonomies for both categories, allowing for a precise count of users who list specific AI skills on their profiles. 

To measure the 'AI skill penetration'—the percentage of the total workforce within a given region or industry—the analysis calculates the proportion of workers who possess the 'AI skills' taxonomy. This is further refined by the process of identifying 'AI-powered' roles. An 'AI-powered' role is identified by analyzing the frequency and prevalence of AI skills within specific job titles, allowing the analysis to move beyond generic job titles to see which roles are being transformed by AI integration. 

Beyond skill tracking, the analysis incorporates the the 'Worldized' dataset and various labor market indices to measure the economic impact and adoption rates of AI across different regions and industries. This allows for a cross-referencing of professional data with regional economic indicators to see how AI adoption correlates with labor market shifts. The tracking of 'AI-related' jobs involves the same taxonomies, but focuses on the same professional categories, specifically identifying the skills required for these roles, such as machine learning (ML), natural language processing (NLP), and the use of specific technical tools like PyTorch and TensorFlow. 

Regarding the professional transition of the workforce, the data reveals a significant shift toward AI-centric roles. There is a a distinct movement of professionals moving from 'non-AI' roles to 'AI-related' roles. This transition is most prevalent among software engineers and data scientists, who are already possess technical foundations that make the transition to AI-centric work more a more intuitive. The data indicates that the demand for these AI skills has significantly outpaced the supply of qualified professionals, which has led to a surge in professional certifications and an increase in self-taught learning as individuals seek to bridge the gap between their existing skills and the current market demand. 

Technically, the categorization of job descriptions into AI and non-AI categories is performed by Large Language Models (LLMs). The methodology describes the technical specifications of the models used for these analyses, including the specific architectural iterations of the LLMs employed to categorize job descriptions. It also specifies the training data's cutoff dates for these LLMs, which is critical for ensuring that the the categorization process is consistent and the data is not influenced by later developments in the AI field that the model might have already 'known' about during its training. This technical grounding ensures that the categorization of roles is based on a technical understanding of the AI field as it existed at a specific point in time, providing a consistent baseline for analyzing the workforce transition.
