# ai-index-report-2025-training-costs-environment-community

**Parent:** [[content/L2/ai-index-2025-technical-ecosystem-synthesis|ai-index-2025-technical-ecosystem-synthesis]] — The AI Index Report 2025 highlights a shift in AI research and patent leadership toward East Asia and the Pacific, alongside a convergence in performance between US and Chinese models, and a significant decrease in inference costs for GPT-3.5 equivalent models from $20 to $0.07 per million2024.

The Artificial Intelligence Index Report 2025, published by the AI Index Steering Committee at Stanford University's Institute for Human-Centered AI, provides a comprehensive analysis of the training costs, hardware requirements, and environmental impact of frontier AI models, alongside an examination of the AI research community and open-source software ecosystem. 

Regarding training costs, the report highlights a significant upward trend. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman stated that training GPT-4 exceeded $100 million, while Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei noted in July 2024 that model training runs costing around $1 billion were already underway. In contrast, DeepSeek-V3 (2024) reportedly cost approximately $6 million to train, though some reports dispute this, arguing that actual development costs were higher when accounting for research expenses, capital expenditures, and employee salaries. To refine these estimates, the AI Index partnered with Epoch AI to analyze training duration, hardware type, quantity, and utilization rates using data from technical reports, press releases, and academic publications. 

Epoch AI's estimates, based on cloud compute rental prices, provide a comparative timeline of training costs: the original Transformer model (2017) cost approximately $670, RoBERTa Large (2019) cost approximately $160,000, OpenAI’s GPT-4 (2023) was estimated at $79 million, and Llama 3.1-405B (2024) had an estimated training cost of $170 million. The AI Index notes a direct correlation between a model's computational requirements and its training cost.

Hardware advancements have been critical in driving AI progress by reducing training time and accelerating complex computations. Machine learning hardware performance, measured in 16-bit floating-point operations, grew at an annual rate of approximately 43% between 2008 and 2024, doubling every 1.9 years. This progress is driven by specialized AI hardware, semiconductor manufacturing advancements, and increased transistor counts. Precision levels are optimized for different use cases: FP32 provides high precision for scientific computing, while lower-precision formats like FP16 and Tensor-FP16/BF16 are optimized for speed and energy efficiency, which is ideal for edge or mobile devices.

Specifically analyzing Nvidia data center GPUs, the H100 GPU (announced March 2022) achieves 22 billion FLOP per second per dollar, which is 1.7 times the price-performance of the A100 (June 2020) and 16.9 times that of the P100 (April 2016). Epoch AI estimates that the cost of hardware with a fixed performance level decreases by 30% annually. In terms of usage, the A100 was the most commonly reported hardware for training notable AI models, used by 64 models, followed by the V100; however, by the end of 2024, 15 models were reported as trained on the H100.

Energy efficiency, measured in FLOP/s per watt, has improved by approximately 40% per year. The Nvidia B100 (March 2024) achieved an energy efficiency of 2.5 trillion FLOP/s per watt, which is 33.8 times more energy efficient than the Nvidia P100 (April 2016), which reported 74 billion FLOP/s per watt. Despite these hardware efficiency gains, the total power consumption required to train frontier AI models is doubling annually due to the trend of training on larger datasets. For example, the original Transformer (2017) consumed an estimated 4,500 watts, PaLM consumed 2.6 million watts, and Llama 3.1-405B (summer 2024) required 25.3 million watts—over 5,000 times the power of the original Transformer.

This increase in power consumption has led to a corresponding rise in carbon emissions. Emissions from training AlexNet (2012) were negligible (0.01 tons of CO2 equivalent), while GPT-3 (2020) emitted around 588 tons of carbon, GPT-4 (2023) emitted 5,184 tons, and Llama 3.1 405B (2024) emitted 8,930 tons. DeepSeek V3 (2024), which has performance comparable to OpenAI’s o1, is estimated to have carbon emissions comparable to GPT-3 (2020). For comparison, the average American emits 18.08 tons of carbon per capita per year, a passenger flight from New York to San Francisco emits 0.99 tons, the average human life emits 5.51 tons per year, and average lifetime car usage (including fuel) emits 63 tons.

To estimate these emissions, the AI Index used developer-published carbon data and supplemented it with a widely used online AI training emissions calculator. This calculator estimates emissions based on training region, cloud provider, total training hours, and hardware type. The AI Index validated the calculator's accuracy by comparing its results with actual emissions reported by developers and found them to be largely consistent.

Beyond the environmental and financial costs of training, the report also analyzes the AI research community and professional networking. AI conferences have grown in scale and prestige over the last two decades. Total attendance at major AI conferences rose by more than 60,000 since 2014, with a 21.7% increase in attendance from 2023 to 2024. Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS) remains the most attended conference, attracting nearly 20,000 participants in 2024. Other conferences with attendance increases in the last year include AAAI, ICLR, IROS, ICRA, ICML, and CVPR. A significant spike in ICML attendance in 2021 was attributed to the conference being held virtually. Total attendance figures include hybrid, virtual, and in-person participation.

Finally, the report examines open-source AI software via GitHub data. The number of AI-related GitHub projects has grown from 1,549 in 2011 to approximately 4.3 million in 2024, with a 40.3% increase in the last year alone. As of 2024, the United States contributes 23.4% of GitHub AI projects, followed by India (19.9%) and Europe (19.5%), though the U.S. share has declined since 2016. China accounts for 2.08% of the contributions. In 2024, the total number of new GitHub stars for AI-related projects rose from 14.0 million in 2023 to 17.7 million in 2024. In terms of cumulative GitHub stars, the United States leads with 21.08 million, followed by the 'Rest of the world' (16.39 million), Europe (10.29 million), India (4.06 million), and China (3.67 million). Popular libraries like PyTorch, Keras, TensorFlow, and OpenCV are among the most starred repositories.
