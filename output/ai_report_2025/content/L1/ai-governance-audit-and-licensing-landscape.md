# ai-governance-audit-and-licensing-landscape

**Parent:** [[content/L2/responsible-ai-incidents-governance-election-data-audits|responsible-ai-incidents-governance-election-data-audits]] — AI incidents surged 56.4% to 233 in 2024 with high-profile cases involving harassment and unauthorized digital recreations, while global policy coordination accelerated through the EU AI Act, OECD updates, and the International Network of AI Safety Institutes. Concurrently, data audits revealed 70% of datasets lack adequate licensing and web restrictions on training data rose from 10% to 48%, as AI election interference impacted four billion voters and multi-agent systems faced infectious jailbreak vulnerabilities.

The contemporary landscape of artificial intelligence research, governance, and data infrastructure is characterized by rapid academic expansion, coordinated international policy development, rigorous empirical auditing of training corpora, and persistent operational uncertainties in system implementation. Academic conference submissions focused on transparency and explainability have experienced a fourfold increase since 2019, culminating in 355 submissions in 2024 across major venues including AAAI, FAccT, AIES, ICML, ICLR, and NeurIPS. This trajectory coincides with a substantial escalation in AI security and safety research, as submissions to select conferences nearly doubled over the preceding year, climbing from 276 to 521. This quantitative surge reflects a pronounced shift in researcher priority toward responsible AI practices, underscoring the field's transition from exploratory development to risk mitigation and governance frameworks.

In parallel with academic trends, global cooperation on AI governance underwent a decisive transformation in 2024, moving away from fragmented national initiatives toward synchronized international frameworks. This coordination began in May 2024 when the OECD updated its AI principles to explicitly emphasize inclusive growth, transparency, explainability, the rule of law, human rights, and democratic values. In the same month, the Council of Europe adopted the Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy, and the Rule of Law, establishing a legally binding treaty that mandates alignment of AI lifecycle activities with democratic values and human rights protections. By June 2024, the European Union enacted the EU AI Act, recognized as the first comprehensive regulatory framework for AI within a major economy. This legislation classifies AI systems by risk level and assigns primary compliance obligations to high-risk system providers or developers. The momentum continued in July 2024 with the African Union's launch of its Continental AI Strategy, which advocates for unified, ethical, responsible, and equitable AI development across the African continent. September 2024 brought the United Nations updated Governing AI for Humanity report, which proposed a blueprint for mitigating AI risks and strongly recommended cross-sector collaboration among standards organizations, technology enterprises, civil society groups, and policymakers. By October 2024, the G7 reaffirmed its dedication to maintaining fair and open AI markets alongside coordinated regulatory approaches, and following the 12th ASEAN-United States Summit, ASEAN and U.S. leaders issued a joint statement committing to cooperative international AI governance frameworks and standards. In November 2024, the International Network of AI Safety Institutes was formally established, uniting nine countries and the European Union to advance AI safety research, deepen the understanding of advanced AI risks, and propose actionable solutions. The timeline concluded in February 2025 with the Arab League launching a dialogue circle on Artificial Intelligence in the Arab World, concentrating on innovative applications and ethical challenges specific to the region.

Within this governance and research environment, the AI Index provides a foundational definition of privacy, framing it as an individual's right to confidentiality, anonymity, and protection of personal data, which includes the right to consent to and be informed about data utilization. This definition simultaneously imposes organizational responsibilities, mandating that entities collecting or using personal data must protect these rights and uphold an individual's right to correct misrepresented information. In practical AI applications, privacy necessitates respectful data handling, protection from exposure, and transparent collection and processing that aligns with regulations such as the GDPR. Organizations operationalize these requirements through data governance, which encompasses the policies, procedures, and standards implemented to guarantee data quality, security, and ethical use, particularly for sensitive or personally identifiable information.

Empirical audits of AI training infrastructure have revealed systemic vulnerabilities in data sourcing and licensing practices. A 2025 large-scale audit conducted by Longpre et al. examined over 1,800 text datasets widely utilized for training foundation models and uncovered pervasive issues in licensing and attribution. More than 70% of datasets hosted on popular platforms lacked adequate license information, and 50% of existing licenses were miscategorized. When the researchers classified licenses into commercial, unspecified, noncommercial, and academic-only categories, they identified sharp discrepancies between their classifications and the metadata reported by GitHub, Papers with Code, and Hugging Face. Such misattribution generates legal and ethical risks, including potential copyright violations, unfair compensation structures for data creators, and model biases arising from excluded licensed data. Furthermore, license inaccuracies hinder transparency, accountability, and reproducibility within AI research, complicating verification and auditing processes for training data.

Concurrently, a longitudinal audit tracking 14,000 web domains used in AI training datasets, specifically C4, RefinedWeb, and Dolma, documented a sharp increase in data use restrictions between 2023 and 2024. Websites increasingly implemented protocols to limit AI training data scraping via robots.txt updates and terms-of-service modifications. The proportion of tokens within the top C4 web domains subject to full restrictions escalated from 10% in 2017 to 48% in 2024, with a concentrated 25 percentage point surge occurring exclusively between 2023 and 2024. Shifting trends in terms-of-service restriction categories across the top C4 domains were also observable from 2016 to 2024. This erosion of consent aligns with ongoing legal disputes over fair use, notably the New York Times lawsuit against OpenAI. OpenAI's web crawlers face the highest restriction levels, whereas smaller developers encounter comparatively fewer barriers. The audit further highlighted inconsistent enforcement driven by ineffective robots.txt signaling and mismatches between stated and enforced policies. These dynamics signal a decline in publicly available web data for AI training, threatening data diversity, model alignment, and scalability, given that recent performance gains have heavily relied on large-scale datasets and restrictive policies may impede future model scaling.
