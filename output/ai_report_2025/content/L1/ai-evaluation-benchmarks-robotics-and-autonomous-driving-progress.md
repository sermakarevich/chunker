# ai-evaluation-benchmarks-robotics-and-autonomous-driving-progress

**Parent:** [[content/L2/ai-index-2025-model-convergence-and-evaluation-landscape|ai-index-2025-model-convergence-and-evaluation-landscape]] — The AI Index Report 2025 documents a period of rapid market convergence and technical scaling, where U.S. private AI investment reached $109.1 billion, model performance gaps between open/closed weights and U.S./Chinese systems collapsed to under 2%, and benchmarks like ARC-AGI saw o3 achieve 87.5% while Waymo's autonomous fleet logged 25.3 million miles with a 92% reduction in injury claims compared to human baselines.

The AI Index Report 2025 presents a comprehensive overview of contemporary artificial intelligence capabilities, emphasizing rigorous evaluation methodologies, rapid advancements in robotics, and the accelerating commercialization and safety validation of autonomous driving systems. The report underscores that assessing true AI competence requires moving beyond static datasets to measure generalized learning, autonomous reasoning, agentic navigation, and real-world deployment readiness across increasingly complex domains.

In the realm of generalized learning, the ARC-AGI benchmark, introduced in 2019 by François Chollet, the creator of the Keras deep-learning framework, remains a critical measure of systems that can efficiently acquire new skills and solve novel problems without prior design or training. ARC-AGI tasks intentionally avoid specialized world knowledge or language dependencies, relying instead on foundational concepts like object permanence, basic topology, and elementary arithmetic that children master early. Because each task introduces unique logic, pre-training for the benchmark is inherently impossible. Initial evaluations in 2020 showed top systems scoring only 20%, which modestly rose to 33% by 2024. However, progress accelerated sharply in 2025 when OpenAI’s o3 model achieved 75.7%, and when allocated a high-compute budget surpassing the benchmark’s standard $10,000 limit, it reached 87.5% on ARC-AGI-1 using the private evaluation set.

As traditional benchmarks approach saturation, researchers behind the MMLU benchmark recently launched Humanity’s Last Exam (HLE), a dataset of 2,700 highly challenging, multimodal questions contributed by leading professors and graduate-level experts across dozens of subjects. These questions were rigorously vetted to resist simple internet lookups, with any question solvable by existing state-of-the-art models discarded before inclusion. Initial testing confirms HLE’s extreme difficulty: OpenAI’s o1 scored just 8.8%, while GPT-4o achieved 3.10%, Grok-2 reached 3.90%, Claude 3.5 Sonnet scored 4.80%, Gemini 1.5 Pro achieved 5.20%, and Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking reached 7.20%. Researchers closely monitoring the trajectory speculate that human-level performance could exceed 50% by the end of 2025.

Evaluation of automated reasoning and planning has also evolved significantly. PlanBench, proposed by Arizona State University and released in 2022, tests AI on 600 problems from the automated planning community, particularly those featured in the International Planning Competition. The benchmark requires models to reason about actions altering hypothetical future states, exemplified by a block-stacking task where a hand can only move one block at a time to a table or the top of a clear block. Early releases showed GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 struggling, but OpenAI’s o1 demonstrated substantial improvements. In the Blocksworld zero-shot evaluation domain, o1 achieved 97.8%, far surpassing Llama 3.1 405B (62.6%) and GPT-4o (35.5%). In the more challenging Mystery Blocksworld domain, where answers are syntactically obfuscated, o1 scored 52.8% compared to 0.8% for Llama 3.1 405B and 0.0% for GPT-4o. Planning remains combinatorial; when tested on instances requiring at least 20 steps, o1 solves only 23.6%, highlighting that reliable planning with long solutions still demands more than linear time. o1’s development marked a deliberate shift toward active reasoning rather than purely autoregressive text prediction.

Agentic capabilities face even steeper hurdles. VisualAgentBench (VAB), launched in 2024, evaluates autonomous or semiautonomous agents across embodied environments (households and gaming), GUI navigation (mobile and web applications), and visual design tasks like CSS debugging. VAB’s results reveal significant deployment gaps: the top-performing model, GPT-4o, achieved only a 36.2% overall success rate. Other systems scored notably lower: GPT-4o-turbo (29.90%), Gemini-1.5-Pro (31.70%), Claude-3.5-Sonnet (26.90%), GPT-4-02 (20.50%), Llama-3.3-70B (21.90%), GPT-4o-mini (19.80%), o1-preview (16.00%), GPT-4-turbo (12.00%), Gemini-2.0-Flash (10.50%), GPT-4 (10.30%), Claude-3.5-Instant (8.90%), GPT-4-01 (8.40%), Llama-3.1-8B (7.70%), and o1-mini (6.30%). Proprietary models average around 20%, with benchmark authors concluding that current AI remains far from direct deployment in agentic settings.

Research and development automation is similarly tested by RE-Bench, launched in 2024 with seven open-ended machine learning research environments informed by data from 71 eight-hour attempts by over 60 human experts. Tasks include optimizing kernels, conducting scaling law experiments, and fine-tuning GPT-2 for question answering. In short time-horizon settings with a two-hour budget, top AI systems outperform human experts by a factor of four. However, as the time budget expands to eight hours, human performance slightly surpasses AI, and at 32 hours, humans outperform AI by a factor of two. Despite this, AI agents already demonstrate human-comparable expertise on certain tasks while delivering results faster and cheaper, such as writing custom Triton kernels.

Generalist assistant capabilities are measured by GAIA, a benchmark introduced by Meta in May 2024 comprising 466 complex, multistep questions requiring reasoning, multimodal processing, web browsing, and tool use. Initial testing showed GPT-4 with plugins correctly answering only 15% of questions compared to 92% for humans. Performance has since accelerated, with the top system in 2024 achieving 65.1%, marking a roughly 30 percentage point increase from the highest 2023 score.

Parallel to software evaluation, the AI Index Report 2025 details transformative progress in robotics and autonomous motion. The robotics community widely adopts RLBench, launched in 2019, which features 100 tasks ranging from simple target reaching to opening an oven and placing a tray inside. Researchers typically evaluate systems on a standardized 18-task subset. As of January 2025, SAM2Act—a collaboration between researchers at the University of Washington, Universidad Católica San Pablo, Nvidia, and the Allen Institute for AI—achieved an 86.8% success rate, improving 2.8 percentage points over the 2024 state-of-the-art and 66.7 percentage points over 2021.

Humanoid robotics matured significantly in 2024. Figure AI launched the Figure 02, standing 5 feet 6 inches tall, weighing 154 pounds, handling a 44-pound payload, and operating for up to five hours on a single charge. The robot performs complex tasks like making coffee and assisting in automotive assembly by placing sheet metal into car fixtures, integrated with OpenAI for speech-to-speech reasoning that allows the robot to explain its actions and respond to queries. This follows Tesla’s Optimus (first launched in 2002 and redesigned in 2023) and Boston Dynamics’ Atlas. DeepMind also advanced robotic foundations, initially launching PaLM-E and RT-2 in 2023, which used transformer architectures trained on both manipulation and language data to excel at text generation and physical control. In 2024, DeepMind introduced AutoRT, an AI system that coordinates multiple video-equipped robots, guides them through environments, devises creative tasks, and documents them for training, generating 77,000 robotic trials spanning 6,650 unique tasks. To address computational bottlenecks, DeepMind developed SARA-RT, using "up-training" to convert transformer quadratic complexity into linear complexity, drastically reducing computational demands while maintaining performance in point cloud and image processing. Coupled with ALOHA and DemoStart methodologies, robots learned tasks like hanging shirts, stacking kitchen items, and tying shoelaces, achieving high success rates on specific tasks including GearInsert-1, GearInsert-2, GearInsert-3, various RandomKitchen combinations, LaceEasy, LaceMessy, ShirtEasy, and ShirtMessy. DemoStart introduced auto-curriculum reinforcement learning using sparse rewards and limited demonstrations. DeepMind also released a 2024 model reaching amateur human-level performance in competitive table tennis.

The industry rapidly adopted robotic foundation models in 2024, following 2023 launches like RT-2, PaLM-E, and Open-X Embodiment. Nvidia introduced GR00T, a general-purpose model for humanoid robots combining natural language understanding with human movement mimicry, alongside data pipelines, simulation frameworks, and the Thor robotics computer. Covariant launched RFM-1, merging language capabilities with real-world maneuverability, while researchers at Stony Brook University and the University of Wisconsin-Madison developed LLaRA, an end-to-end deep learning model integrating perception, communication, and action.

Autonomous driving continues its steady commercial and technical expansion. Waymo, an Alphabet subsidiary, launched robotaxis in Phoenix in early 2022 and expanded to San Francisco in 2024. As of January 2025, Waymo operates in Phoenix, San Francisco, Los Angeles, and Austin, providing 150,000 paid rides per week and covering over a million miles. Rider-only miles driven without a human driver through September 2024 totaled 1.947 million in Los Angeles, 10.209 million in San Francisco, 20.823 million in Phoenix, and 124,000 in Austin. Waymo plans to test in 10 additional cities, including Las Vegas, San Diego, Miami, upstate New York, and Truckee, California, strategically selecting snowy regions to evaluate performance in diverse conditions. Conversely, GM’s Cruise launched autonomous vehicles in San Francisco in late 2022 before having its license suspended in 2023 following safety incidents.

China’s autonomous sector accelerates rapidly. Baidu’s Apollo Go reported 988,000 rides across China in Q3 2024, a 20% year-over-year increase, operating 400 robotaxis in October 2024 with plans to reach 1,000 by year-end. Pony.AI aims to scale its fleet from 200 to at least 1,000 vehicles by end of 2026, anticipating 2,000 to 3,000 total. China leads global autonomous vehicle testing, deploying driverless cars across 16 cities at prices often cheaper than human-driven alternatives, supported by prioritized national regulations. European startups like Wayve are also gaining traction. In commercial freight, Kodiak completed its first driverless deliveries, while Aurora reported over 1 million miles of autonomous freight hauling on U.S. highways since 2021, though it delayed its commercial fleet launch to April 2025. Technological hardware innovations in October 2024 included Tesla’s Cybercab (a two-passenger autonomous vehicle without a steering wheel or pedals, slated for 2026 production under $30,000), Tesla’s Robovan (a 20-passenger electric autonomous van), and Baidu’s latest-generation RT6 robotaxi.

Cost reduction and rigorous evaluation frameworks underpin autonomous scaling. The Baidu Apollo Go RT6 robotaxi features a $30,000 price tag and a battery-swapping system, targeting scalable affordability. Business partnerships like Uber’s collaboration with WeRide (the world’s first publicly listed robotaxi company) are developing autonomous ride-sharing platforms in Abu Dhabi. To overcome open-loop evaluation limitations that cause memorization rather than authentic learning, 2024 introduced nuPlan (by Motional), featuring 1,282 hours of diverse driving scenarios and a closed-loop simulation framework; Bench2Drive, providing over 2 million annotated frames from 10,000 clips and 220 evaluation routes measuring route completion and infractions; and OpenAD, the first real-world, open-world 3D object detection benchmark focusing on domain generalization and open-vocabulary recognition.

Safety validation remains paramount. Waymo’s collaboration with reinsurer Swiss Re compared liability claims over millions of miles against human baselines (drawing from Swiss Re’s 500,000+ claims and 200 billion miles of data). Waymo vehicles showed an 88% reduction in property damage claims and a 92% reduction in bodily injury claims. Specifically, across 25.3 million miles, Waymo incurred just nine property damage claims and two bodily injury claims, whereas human drivers would be expected to incur 78 and 26 respectively. Waymo vehicles also recorded 1.42 fewer airbag deployments, 3.16 fewer injury-reported crashes, and 3.65 fewer police-reported crashes per million miles compared to human equivalents across Phoenix and San Francisco, consistently outperforming latest-generation human-driven vehicles equipped with modern safety features.
