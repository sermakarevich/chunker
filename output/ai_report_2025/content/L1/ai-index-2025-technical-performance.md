# ai-index-2025-technical-performance

**Parent:** [[content/L2/ai-technical-performance-2025|ai-technical-performance-2025]] — The 2025 AI Index Report analyzes the convergence of frontier models, noting that o3 achieved an 87.5% score on ARC-AGI and o1 solved 74.4% of IMO qualifying exams, while the gap between US and Chinese models narrowed to 1.7% on the LMSYS Chatbot Arena.

The Technical Performance chapter of the Artificial Intelligence Index Report 2025, published by the AI Index Steering Committee at Stanford University's Institute for Human-Centered AI, provides a comprehensive analysis of AI model performance across coding, mathematics, reasoning, and agentic capabilities. 

### AI Performance in Coding and Mathematics

In the domain of coding, the LMSYS Chatbot Arena for LLMs coding filter indicates that among Chinese models, DeepSeek-V3 leads with an Elo score of 1,317, trailing the highest-ranking model by 3.8%.

Mathematical problem-solving is evaluated using benchmarks that span from grade-school to competition-level mathematics. The GSM8K benchmark, introduced by OpenAI in 2021, contains approximately 8,000 diverse grade-school math word problems requiring multistep arithmetic solutions. The top-performing model on GSM8K is a variant of Claude Sonnet 3.5 optimized using the HPT prompting strategy, which achieved a score of 97.72%, a significant increase from the 2023 high of 91.00%. However, in 2024, several models from Mistral, Meta, and Qwen scored around 96%, suggesting that the GSM8K benchmark may be approaching saturation.

For more challenging tasks, the MATH dataset, introduced in 2021 by UC Berkeley and University of Chicago researchers, contains 12,500 competition-level mathematics problems. While AI systems initially solved only 6.9% of these problems, OpenAI’s o3-mini (high) model achieved a record score of 97.9% in January 2025, surpassing the human baseline of 90%.

Public preference in mathematics is tracked via the Chatbot Arena's math filter, which has collected over 340,000 public votes across 181 models. While Gemini-based models lead in the general and coding arenas, the top-ranked model in the Math Arena is an OpenAI o1 variant released in December 2024.

To address the saturation of high school and college-level benchmarks, Epoch AI introduced FrontierMath. This benchmark features hundreds of original, exceptionally challenging problems in areas such as number theory, real analysis, algebraic geometry, and category theory. Upon initial release, Gemini 1.5 Pro was the best-performing model but solved only 2.0% of the problems; other leading models, including o1-preview, o1-mini, GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Grok 2 Beta, also showed very low success rates. However, OpenAI’s o3 model is reported to have scored 25.2% on FrontierMath.

In the area of theorem proving, Google DeepMind developed AlphaProof and AlphaGeometry 2 for the 2024 International Mathematical Olympiad (IMO). AlphaProof, a reinforcement learning system derived from AlphaZero, uses a fine-tuned Gemini model to translate natural language problems into the Lean formal proof system. AlphaGeometry 2 is a neuro-symbolic hybrid system using a Gemini-based language model trained on synthetic data. Together, they solved four out of six problems in the 2024 IMO: AlphaProof solved two algebra problems and one number theory problem, while AlphaGeometry 2 solved the sole geometry problem in 24 seconds. This performance level is equivalent to an IMO silver medalist, who typically solves 22.9 geometry problems in the IMO-AG-30 benchmarking set, whereas AlphaGeometry solved 25 out of 30 problems.

### AI Reasoning and General Intelligence

As older benchmarks like SQuAD and VQA have become saturated, researchers from the United States and Canada developed the Massive Multi-discipline Multimodal Understanding and Reasoning Benchmark (MMMU). MMMU consists of approximately 11,500 college-level questions across six core disciplines: art and design, business, science, health and medicine, humanities and social science, and technology and engineering. As of January 2025, OpenAI’s o1 is the highest-performing model on MMMU with a score of 78.2%, improving upon the state-of-the-art score of 59.4% reported in the 2024 AI Index, though it remains below the human expert baseline of 82.6%.

Regarding general, multisubject AI reasoning, researchers from NYU, Anthropic, and Meta introduced the GPQA benchmark to test AI systems. The GPQA dataset contains 448 difficult multiple-choice questions crafted by subject-matter experts in biology, physics, and chemistry, designed to be resistant to simple web searches. In the 2023 AI Index, the best-performing model, GPT-4, achieved a score of 38.8% on the GPQA diamond test set (the most challenging subset). By December 2024, OpenAI's o3 model achieved a state-of-the-art score of 87.7% on the diamond set, representing a 48.9 percentage point improvement over the 2023 state-of-the-art and becoming the first model to exceed the baseline accuracy of 81.3% set by expert human validators.

To evaluate the ability of AI systems to generalize beyond prior training, François Chollet (creator of the Keras deep learning library) introduced the ARC-AGI benchmark in 2019. ARC-AGI presents AI systems with independent tasks featuring demonstration or input pairs followed by test or output scenarios, requiring assumed prior knowledge (such as basic topology, objects, and elementary arithmetic) rather than specialized world knowledge. In 2020, the top-performing system on ARC-AGI scored 20%, and by 2024, the score rose to 33%. However, OpenAI's o3 model achieved a score of 75.7%, and when allocated a high compute budget exceeding the benchmark's $10,000 limit, it reached a score of 87.5%. Researchers suggest that previous slow progress was due to an overemphasis on scaling model size and training data, whereas recent gains indicate a shift toward improving search capabilities and generalization.

Due to the saturation of benchmarks like MMLU, GSM8K, and HumanEval, the team behind MMLU introduced Humanity's Last Exam (HLE), a benchmark comprising 2,700 highly challenging multimodal questions across dozens of subject areas. These questions were contributed by leading professors and graduate-level reviewers and were specifically tested against state-of-the-art LLMs before inclusion to ensure no existing model could answer them. Initial testing shows HLE is extremely difficult; OpenAI's o1 scores only 8.8%, while Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking (7.2%), Gemini 1.5 Pro (5.2%), Claude 3.5 Sonnet (4.8%), Grok-2 (3.9%), and GPT-4o (3.1%) score even lower. Researchers speculate that performance could exceed 50% by the end of 2025.

### Planning and Agentic Capabilities

In the domain of planning—reasoning about actions that alter the world—Arizona State University proposed PlanBench, a suite of 600 problems based on the International Planning Competition. PlanBench tests models on tasks such as constructing stacks of blocks (Blocksworld), where a hand can only move one block at a time. In the Blocksworld zero-shot evaluation, OpenAI's o1 scored 97.8%, significantly outperforming Llama 3.1 405B (62.6%) and GPT-4o (35.5%). In the more difficult Mystery Blocksworld domain, where answers are syntactically obfuscated, o1 scored 52.8%, while Llama 3.1 405B scored 0.8% and GPT-4 scored 0%. Despite these gains, o1 solves only 23.6% of planning instances that require at least 20 steps.

Additionally, the report discusses AI agents—autonomous or semiautonomous systems designed to accomplish goals in specific environments. Because agentic tasks are diverse and dynamic, they are harder to benchmark than image classification. In 2024, VisualAgentBench (VAB) was launched to test agents in three categories: embodied agents (household and gaming), GUI agents (mobile and web apps), and GraphQL design agents (CSS debugging). On the VAB test set, GPT-4o achieved the highest overall success rate at 36.2%, while most proprietary models averaged around 20%.

To test the R&D capabilities of AI systems, researchers launched RE-Bench in 2024, featuring seven open-ended machine learning research environments, such as optimizing a kernel or fine-tuning GPT-2. In short time-horizon settings (two-hour budget), the best AI systems score four times higher than human experts. In a 32-hour budget setting, however, humans outperform AI by a factor of two. In certain tasks, such as writing custom Triton kernels, AI agents can complete the work more quickly than any human expert.
