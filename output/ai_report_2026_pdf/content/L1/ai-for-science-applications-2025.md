# ai-for-science-applications-2025

**Parent:** [[content/L2/ai-landscape-2025-synthesis|ai-landscape-2025-synthesis]] — In 2025, the AI landscape is marked by US capital dominance, a shift toward 'Vertical AI' and 'AI4Science', and an RAI framework maturity increase from 2.5 to 2.8, despite a persistence of technical failures like the Pydantic 'model_dump' AttributeError.

The integration of AI into scientific research in 2025 is characterized by a transition from AI as a simple data analysis tool to a collaborative partner in the hypothesis-generation process. This shift is formalised as the 'AI-for-Science' (AI4Science) paradigm, AI models are now trained specifically on scientific data rather than general text. Models are being used to narrow the search space for new hypotheses, accelerating discovery in materials science, drug development, and physics. In physics, AI is helping to analyze massive datasets from particle accelerators and telescopes that would be impossible for humans to process manually. In materials science, AI suggests thousands of candidate materials, which are then validated by human researchers in a lab.

Within the biological and health sciences, there is a significant focus on the intersection of AI and genomics. AI models are used to predict protein folding and genomic sequences, which directly accelerates drug discovery. This is achieved through the application of 'protein languages,' where LLMs treat amino acid sequences as tokens to predict functional sites and mutations. Furthermore, the application of geometric deep learning (GDL) has enabled more precise modeling of 3D molecular structures. This is critical for understanding how small molecules bind to proteins, which in turn improves the accuracy of virtual screening processes.

There is a notable trend toward the use of smaller, specialized models that can outperform larger, general-purpose models in molecular and biological discovery. To further enhance capabilities, there is a growing trend toward integrating multimodal data—combining text, image, and genomic data—to improve the ability to predict protein functions and drug-target interactions. In biological research, the 'Open-set' approach is specifically utilized to analyze cellular behavior.

In the medical sector, AI application is characterized by a tension between technological promise and real-world implementation. While AI is being applied to clinical tasks, such as clinical decision support and the automation of diagnostic tasks, there remains a significant gap in the actual deployment of these tools in real-world medical settings and healthcare workflows. Consequently, there is a a heavy emphasis on the necessity of evaluating these models for safety, reliability, and clinical efficacy before widespread adoption.

In the realm of earth sciences, AI is integrated into climate modeling to bridge the gap between coarse-grained global climate models and high-resolution local weather patterns. This is accomplished via machine learning 'emulators' which replace computationally expensive physics-based simulations, allowing for faster and more frequent climate projections. Additionally, AI is utilized in satellite imagery analysis to track deforestation and urban sprawl in real-time.

Crucially, the 'AI-for-Science' paradigm is being operationalized through the development of closed-loop systems. These systems integrate AI with robotics, such as lab-on-a-chip systems, creating a 'predict-synthesize-test-analyze' cycle. In this loop, the AI proposes a hypothesis, robots conduct the experiment, and the results are fed back into the AI to refine the model. This has led to the emergence of 'AI scientists'—agents capable of hypothesis generation and experimental design—which has significantly reduced the time required for lead optimization in drug discovery compared to traditional manual methods.
